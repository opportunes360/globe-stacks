An interview invitation is good news. It means the panel has read your papers and wants to meet the person behind them. Most students feel nervous, and that is normal. This guide shows you what to expect and how to get ready, step by step.

> **Please read:** not every scholarship has an interview, and the format changes from one to the next. Always follow the instructions in **your own invitation**. Where this guide talks about a specific scholarship, it only repeats what is in that scholarship's entry on this site, and it says "not confirmed" when we could not read it on an official page.

## What an interview is for

The panel already has your grades, your essay and your letters. The interview answers different questions:

- **Is this really you?** Do your answers match your application?
- **Do you know why you want this?** Course, country, scholarship.
- **How do you think and speak?** Can you explain your ideas clearly?

It is a conversation, not a trap.

## What interviews look like for scholarships on this site

Formats are very different, so do not assume. Here is what we know from the entries.

- **Türkiye Burslari.** The official page describes a face-to-face interview with a committee of academics and experts. It usually lasts **15 to 30 minutes**. The order is: welcome, document check, why you applied, your academic knowledge and career goals, and finally your questions to the committee. For undergraduates there is also a 30-question maths, geometry and logic test on the **same day**. You must bring the original documents and certificates of your latest education. The official calendar puts interviews between **April and June**. Read the full [Türkiye Burslari entry](/scholarships/turkiye-burslari/).
- **GKS Korea.** After checking your documents, the committee holds an **oral interview**. We did not find a Tanzania-specific 2027 notice, so the interview dates and format for Tanzania are **not confirmed**. See the [GKS Korea entry](/scholarships/gks-undergraduate-korea/).
- **MEXT Japan.** In the last round run by the Japanese Embassy in Tanzania, the written exam and interview were in person, on 3 June and 23 June 2026 (both "subject to change"). The next round's dates are not announced. See the [MEXT entry](/scholarships/mext-japan-undergraduate/).
- **UBC International Scholars.** Some applicants are invited to a **video interview** and get **one week to record their answers**. The number of questions and the time limit are **not confirmed** on the page we read. See the [UBC entry](/scholarships/ubc-international-scholars/).
- **Oxford.** Oxford says its interviews are academic conversations about your chosen subject, a bit like a short tutorial, held online on Microsoft Teams. In some subjects they can draw on your personal statement or written work. Whether the Reach Oxford Scholarship itself has a separate interview is **not confirmed**. See the [Reach Oxford entry](/scholarships/reach-oxford-scholarship/).
- **China CSC.** Some countries run an embassy interview after nomination. Whether Tanzania does is **not confirmed**. See the [CSC entry](/scholarships/chinese-government-scholarship/).
- **Dartmouth.** An alumni interview is optional and only happens if an alum contacts you. Interviews are not required. See the [Dartmouth entry](/scholarships/dartmouth-college/).

The pattern: some interviews are in person, some are live online, some are recorded, and some do not exist. Your invitation is the only source that counts.

## Start preparing before the invitation

Invitations can come with short notice, so start early.

1. **Reread your own application.** Your personal statement, CV and study plan. The panel will have them in front of them. If you wrote that you led a project, be ready to explain it for two minutes. Our guide on [writing a personal statement](/guides/how-to-write-a-personal-statement/) will help you remember what you promised.
2. **Know your course.** Be able to name two or three subjects you will study and why they interest you.
3. **Know the scholarship and the country.** Read the official page again. Know what it pays for and what it asks of you.
4. **Keep up with your subject.** UCAS, the UK university application service, says you will not be tested on the latest news in your field, but reading about it shows real interest.

## Common questions, with sample answers

These samples are **examples we wrote**, not real student answers and not taken from any panel. Do not copy them. Use them to see the shape of a good answer, then fill it with your own true story.

### 1. "Tell me about yourself."

Keep it to about a minute: who you are now, one thing that shaped you, and where you want to go.

> **Example:** "I am Neema, I finished Form Six this year at a government school in Dodoma. I study physics, chemistry and mathematics. When our school got a broken solar panel donated, I helped our science teacher repair it, and that made me want to study electrical engineering. I would like to learn how to build cheap, reliable power systems for villages like mine."

**Why it works:** it is short, true, specific, and it ends with a goal.

### 2. "Why did you choose this course?"

Give a real reason and one proof that you acted on it.

> **Example:** "I want to study nursing because my aunt was sick for months and the only nurse at our clinic looked after forty people a day. I volunteered at the clinic during holidays for two years. I saw how much a good nurse changes things. I want to be that nurse, and later to train others."

### 3. "Why this country, university or scholarship?"

Say something specific you learned from the official pages. Do not only say the country is "beautiful" or "developed".

> **Example:** "I read that the engineering programme includes a year of practical work with local companies. That fits how I learn best. I also read that the scholarship expects students to contribute to society, and my work with the school science club taught me to share what I learn."

If you cannot name anything specific, go back to the official page and read it again before the interview.

### 4. "Tell me about a challenge you faced."

Use three steps: **what happened, what you did, what you learned.**

> **Example:** "In Form Five I had no textbook for chemistry. I borrowed a friend's book each evening, copied the key pages by hand, and formed a study group of four. My marks went from 45 to 71. I learned that a problem is easier when you ask for help early."

### 5. "What are your plans after graduation?"

Be realistic. Many scholarships care about how you will use your education.

> **Example:** "I plan to work for two or three years to gain experience, then return to Tanzania to work in renewable energy. I would also like to teach science students on weekends, as my teacher did for me."

### 6. "Do you have any questions for us?"

Always have one or two. Ask something you could not find on the official page. UCAS advises asking thoughtful questions from your research of the course guide and the university website, because it shows interest. Do not ask something the page already answers.

> **Example:** "Are there student societies or projects that first-year students can join?"

### 7. Subject questions

Some interviews, like Türkiye Burslari and Oxford, test how you think about your subject. If you do not know an answer:

- **Think aloud.** Say your reasoning step by step. Oxford says interviews are meant to see how you think and respond to new ideas.
- **It is fine to pause.** A few quiet seconds to think is better than a rushed wrong answer.
- **Be honest.** "I am not sure, but I would start by..." is a strong answer.

## Online interviews

Many interviews are now online, and some are recorded. Our advice, based on common sense and what universities say, not an official rule:

- **Test early.** Test your phone or laptop, camera, microphone and the app the panel uses (Oxford, for example, uses Microsoft Teams) at least a day before. Do it again an hour before.
- **Plan for power and data.** Charge your device and have a power bank. Buy enough data. If possible, ask a teacher, library or school for a quiet room with reliable internet.
- **Check the time.** Convert the time in your invitation to **East Africa Time** and write it down twice. Time zone mistakes cost people their interviews.
- **Look at the camera**, not at your own face. UCAS says to sit up straight and look alert, whether the interview is online or in person.
- **If the connection fails,** stay calm, reconnect, and contact the organisers using the details in your invitation.

### If the interview is recorded

Recorded interviews feel strange because nobody reacts. Practise by recording yourself on your phone. Use short notes, not a full script. Whether you get a second try is **not confirmed** for UBC, so read your instructions.

## In person, and what to wear

- **Arrive early.** Know the address, the room and the travel time.
- **Bring your documents:** originals and copies, in a clean folder.
- **Greet politely,** listen to the whole question, and say thank you at the end.
- **Dress neatly.** We found no dress code for the scholarships on this site, so our advice is clean, simple clothes you are comfortable in. They do not need to be expensive.
- Switch your phone to silent. Speak clearly and a little slower than normal.
- If your English is not perfect, that is fine. If you do not understand a question, say: "Could you please repeat that?"

## Practise with other people

1. **Ask a teacher** or an older student to be the panel.
2. **Practise out loud.** Thinking an answer is not the same as saying it.
3. **Do not memorise scripts.** Learn your key points and say them in your own words.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Saying things that are not true.** Your answers should match your documents.
- **Not knowing your own application.** Reread it.
- **Speaking badly of your school, teachers, country or other applicants.**
- **Giving one-word answers,** or answers that go on and on. Aim for 30 seconds to two minutes.
- **Arriving late** or logging in late, or forgetting your documents.
- **Giving up after one weak answer.** Take a breath and continue.
- **Paying someone** who promises to "guarantee" you pass. Nobody can. Read [how to spot scams](/guides/how-scholarships-work-and-how-to-spot-scams/).

## After the interview

- Keep your phone and email active, and use the contact details in your invitation if you have a real question.
- In the last MEXT round the embassy did not give reasons for unsuccessful applications.
- If you are selected, a visa interview may come later. It is a different interview, so prepare for it separately: see [student visa basics](/guides/student-visa-basics/).

## Quick checklist

- [ ] I reread my personal statement and CV.
- [ ] I can say why I chose my course, country and scholarship, with specific details.
- [ ] I practised answers out loud with someone.
- [ ] I know the time, place or link, in East Africa Time.
- [ ] My documents are ready, originals and copies.
- [ ] My device, internet and power are checked (for online).
- [ ] I have one or two questions to ask the panel.

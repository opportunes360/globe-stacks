> **Please read:** the Tanzanian rules below come from official pages, but some of those pages are old (one is from 2018). Fees, offices and opening days change. **Before you pay or travel, phone or email the Ministry of Foreign Affairs and East African Cooperation (nje@nje.go.tz, +255 26 2323201-7) and ask the scholarship or university what exactly it wants.**

Many scholarships say things like "send certified copies", "translate your certificates" or "documents must be apostilled". These words sound scary, but each one has a simple meaning. This guide explains them in plain English and shows how it works for Tanzanian students.

## The short version

- **Certified copy:** a photocopy that an authorised person has checked against your original and signed or stamped to say "this copy is true".
- **Certified translation:** a translation done by a recognised translator, so the reader can trust it matches the original.
- **Legalisation:** a chain of stamps that tells another country "this signature and seal are real". An **apostille** is a faster, one-stamp version of this, but only between countries that joined a special treaty.

You do not need all three for every scholarship. Read the checklist of each scholarship first, then do only what it asks for.

## 1. Certified copies

A normal photocopy proves nothing, because anyone can copy anything. A certified copy has a person's signature and stamp on it. That person looked at your original and agreed the copy is the same.

**Who can certify a copy in Mainland Tanzania?** The Notaries Public and Commissioners for Oaths Act (Cap. 12) says:

- An **advocate** can practise as a notary public and commissioner for oaths, once he or she holds a certificate from the Registrar of the High Court. That certificate ends every 31 December unless it is renewed.
- Some public officers have the same power to certify copies, including **magistrates, the Registrars of the High Court and Court of Appeal, and Administrative Officers** in government service.

**What does it cost?** The Act's fee table (the version we read is dated 30 November 2019) lists **Tshs 20,000** for certifying a copy of up to 100 words, and **Tshs 5,000** for each extra 100 words. Fees can change, so ask the price before the person starts stamping. Fees collected by government officers go to government revenue.

**How to do it:**

1. Bring your **original** document and a clear copy. Never hand over your only original to a stranger.
2. Ask for the certifier's stamp, signature and date on every page that needs it.
3. Check that your name, date of birth and spelling match your other documents.
4. Keep a scan of everything before you post any papers.

**Zanzibar:** the Act we read says it covers Mainland Tanzania. We did **not confirm** who certifies copies in Zanzibar.

## 2. Translations

If a document is already in English, you normally do not need an English translation. You need one when a paper is in another language, for example a letter in Swahili.

What we confirmed:

- The Tanzanian Ministry of Foreign Affairs says that documents in a language other than Kiswahili or English must be translated into English or Kiswahili, and the translation must come together with the original. It says translations should be done by **BAKITA, BAKWATA or another institution the Government recognises**.
- **Yale** asks for a certified translation of any document that is not in English and does not accept translations you make yourself (see the [Yale entry](/scholarships/yale-university/)).
- The Tanzanian Embassy in Ankara says documents for use in Turkey must be translated into Turkish by a certified translator and notary. This shows that the **country you are going to** can have its own rule. We did not confirm which scholarships need a Turkish translation, so ask the scholarship.

**Tip:** do not translate your own documents unless the scholarship says that is allowed. Ask first which language is needed and who may do it. A translation in the wrong language is money wasted.

## 3. Legalisation and apostille

This is the part that confuses most people. It answers one question: "How can an office in another country know that the Tanzanian stamp on my paper is real?"

**Apostille.** Countries that joined the Hague Apostille Convention agree that one stamp, the apostille, is enough. The convention's official status table (last updated 30 June 2026) lists 130 contracting parties. **Tanzania is not on that list.** So we found no apostille route for Tanzanian documents. If a scholarship says "apostilled", ask the university or embassy what Tanzanian students should do instead. The usual answer is the legalisation chain below.

**Legalisation (the chain).** The Tanzanian Ministry of Foreign Affairs and its embassies describe it like this:

1. **The issuing office verifies the document first.** The Ministry says every document must first be verified by the office that issued it. For marriage, divorce, birth and death papers that office is **RITA** (the Registration, Insolvency and Trusteeship Agency). For exam results it is **NECTA**. If you lost your certificate, NECTA's eServices site offers a replacement certificate or a statement of results, depending on the year you sat the exam.
2. **The Ministry certifies the document.** The Ministry's page says it can legalise birth certificates, death certificates and educational documents. The 2018 notice says you write a letter to the Permanent Secretary, bring each copy together with its original, pay by bank slip, and have the Ministry's Legal Unit check the papers before payment.
3. **The embassy of the country you are going to confirms the Ministry's stamp.** For example, the Danish and Belgian embassies in Dar es Salaam describe the same two steps: the Ministry first, then their own embassy.

**Zanzibar documents:** the Tanzanian Embassy in Ankara says Zanzibar documents are first legalised by the Ministry's department in Zanzibar and then by the Ministry in the mainland.

**What we could not confirm:**

- The 2018 notice gives the Ministry's fee as **Tshs 15,000 per copy** and the service days as **Tuesday and Thursday, 9:00 to 12:00**. The notice is old, so treat both as **not confirmed today**.
- The 2018 notice names the Ministry's offices in Dodoma. The Ministry's website now gives its address as Government City, Mtumba, Dodoma. We did not confirm whether any other office still handles this, or whether you can apply online.
- **How long it takes: not confirmed.** We found no official processing time. Plan for weeks, not days.

## Your step-by-step plan

1. **Read the scholarship's document list** and write down exactly which papers need certifying, translating or legalising.
2. **Collect your originals early:** certificates from NECTA, your birth certificate, your [passport](/guides/getting-your-passport-in-tanzania/). Fix spelling mistakes first.
3. **Get certified copies** from an advocate who is a notary public, or another authorised officer.
4. **Translate** only what is in another language, using a recognised translator.
5. **Verify** with NECTA or RITA, then take the papers to the **Ministry** for legalisation.
6. **Go to the embassy** if the destination country asks for it.
7. **Scan everything**, and keep one paper set and one digital set.

Do this **before** the call opens, if you can. Many deadlines leave only a few weeks. See also our guide to [student visas](/guides/student-visa-basics/), which uses many of the same papers.

## Mistakes and scams to avoid

- **Paying a "fixer".** The Ministry's notice says payment is made by bank slip into a government account, so be careful with anyone who asks for cash or a phone transfer. Check the official payment method first.
- **Skipping a step.** The Ministry says the issuing office must verify a document before it is brought for certification, so do not skip that step.
- **Names that do not match** across passport, certificates and translations.
- **Certifying too early.** Some papers must be recent. For example, our China entry says the non-criminal record certificate is usually issued within six months before you submit. Read the date rule before you start.
- **Laminating.** Some embassies say not to laminate documents. Keep them flat in a folder.

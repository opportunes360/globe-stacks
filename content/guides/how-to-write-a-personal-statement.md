A personal statement (also called a statement of purpose or motivation letter) is your chance to be a real person on paper. The people reading it have read hundreds. Yours should help them remember you.

## What readers want to know

Almost every scholarship panel is quietly asking four questions:

1. **Who are you?** What has shaped you?
2. **Why this course?** What made you choose it?
3. **Why this scholarship, university or country?** Why here?
4. **What will you do with it?** How will your education help other people?

If your essay answers these four honestly and clearly, you are already ahead of most applicants.

## A structure you can follow

Aim for **one page to one and a half pages** unless the instructions say otherwise. Always follow the word limit.

### 1. Open with a real moment

Start with a short, specific moment, not a general statement. Compare:

- **Weak:** "I have always been passionate about medicine."
- **Stronger:** "When my neighbour's child was carried past our house at night because the clinic was a two-hour walk away, I decided I would be the person who made that walk shorter."

Use your own true story. It does not need to be dramatic. It needs to be real.

### 2. Show what you have done

Give two or three examples that prove your interest: a school club you led, a project you built, a job you did, helping your family, or learning something on your own. Say **what you did and what changed because of it.**

### 3. Explain why this course and this place

Name something specific: a subject in the course, a project, or something about the programme that fits your goals. This shows you did your homework, and it separates you from people who send the same essay everywhere.

### 4. Say what you will do next

Describe your plan after graduation and how it will help your community or country. Be realistic and concrete.

### 5. End simply

Finish with one or two confident sentences. No dramatic pleading.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Copying.** Selection panels check for copied or generated text. Write your own words. Using a sample as inspiration is fine, but copying it is not.
- **Clichés.** "Since childhood I have dreamed..." appears in thousands of essays.
- **Listing instead of showing.** Do not say "I am hardworking". Show it with an example.
- **Hiding your background.** If you studied with little money or little equipment, say so plainly. That is part of your story, not something to be embarrassed about.
- **Spelling and grammar mistakes.** Read your essay aloud, and ask a teacher to read it.
- **Making it too long.** Respect the limit.

## Before and after

**Before:** "I am a hardworking student who wants to study engineering because I like technology."

**After:** "In Form Three I repaired our school's broken projector using parts from an old radio. Three classes could watch science videos again. That is when I knew I wanted to study engineering: I like fixing things that help many people at once."

The second version names a real event, says what changed, and explains why.

## Checklist before you send

- [ ] Does it answer the four questions?
- [ ] Is the first sentence about a real moment?
- [ ] Do I have at least two examples with results?
- [ ] Did I mention something specific about this programme?
- [ ] Is it within the word limit?
- [ ] Did someone else read it?
- [ ] Is it all my own writing?

## Free tools

The Purdue Online Writing Lab (owl.purdue.edu) has free guides on writing and grammar. Use it for ideas, but keep your own voice.

Some of the best scholarships for African students are not called scholarships at all. At colleges like Yale, Dartmouth and Amherst, you apply for **financial aid**. This guide explains the words and the forms, so you are not confused when you see them.

> **Good to know:** Everything here comes from the colleges' own aid pages as we read them on 19 November 2026. Rules change every year. The college's page always wins over this guide.

## The big idea

Most scholarships you know are **merit scholarships**. You win them because of your results or talent.

Need-based aid is different. The college looks at **how much your family can pay**. Then it gives you the difference as a grant. A grant is money you do **not** pay back.

So at these colleges:

- Strong marks and activities get you **admitted**.
- Your family's finances decide **how much aid** you get.

## Key words

### Need-blind
The college decides whether to admit you **without looking at your family's money**. Asking for aid does not lower your chance.

Yale, Dartmouth and Amherst all say their admission is need-blind for international students. Read each college's page to confirm, because many colleges do not do this for international students.

### Need-aware
The college **does look** at whether you need aid when it decides. If it has a small aid budget, asking for aid can lower your chance. Many colleges work this way. Always check which kind a college is before you apply.

### Demonstrated need
The college takes the full yearly cost of attending and subtracts what it thinks your family can pay. The difference is your "demonstrated need".

Yale, Dartmouth and Amherst say they meet **100% of demonstrated need**. In simple words: if you are admitted, they give you enough aid to cover the gap. They say this is given as grants, not loans.

> **Careful:** "100% of need" does not mean "everything is free". If the college decides your family can pay something, you must pay that part. Check what is counted in the cost, for example whether flights are included. Each entry on this site says what we confirmed.

## The three forms you may meet

### 1. The college's own application
For admission. At most colleges you apply through the **Common Application** or the college's own system. There is usually an application fee. Many colleges let you ask for a **fee waiver** if the fee would cause hardship. Asking does not hurt your application, according to Dartmouth.

### 2. The CSS Profile
An online form run by the College Board. It asks about your family's income, assets and costs. Many colleges use it to decide your aid.

Important facts from the college pages we read:

- Each college has a **code**. For example, Dartmouth's CSS code is 3351 and Amherst's is 3003. You add the codes of the colleges you apply to.
- Amherst says to report your family's money **in your home currency** (for a Tanzanian family, shillings).
- If your parents are **separated or divorced**, Amherst says each parent fills in their own form.
- The CSS Profile **costs money**. We could not read the current price on the College Board's site, so we do not quote one. Check it before you start.

> **Careful:** Do **not** pay for the CSS Profile until your college asks for it. Yale's page says international applicants do not have to send it unless Yale asks. Paying early can waste money.

### 3. The ISFAA
The **International Student Financial Aid Application**. It is an alternative to the CSS Profile for families who cannot afford the CSS fee.

- Dartmouth says it does not give CSS fee waivers. If the fee is a serious hardship, you can complete the ISFAA instead and send it by email.
- Amherst treats the ISFAA the same as the CSS Profile. You ask for it by logging into your Amherst admission account.
- Yale says it does **not** accept the ISFAA instead of its own forms. It reviews requests for a CSS payment code instead.

Different colleges, different rules. This is why you must read each college's page.

## How to apply for aid, step by step

1. **Make a list of colleges and note, for each one:** need-blind or not, which form it wants (CSS, ISFAA or its own), the aid deadline, and whether documents must be in English.
2. **Gather your family documents early.** Colleges usually ask for proof of income. Common items are tax records, salary letters, or a statement that your parents have no formal income. Each college lists what it accepts. Ask them if something does not apply to your family.
3. **Get certified translations** if documents are not in English. Yale says it does not accept translations you make yourself. Dartmouth's page does not say whether translations are needed, so ask before you pay.
4. **Say you want aid on your admission application.** Do it at the same time. Do not wait until after you are admitted.
5. **Wait for the college's email** with its aid portal instructions before uploading documents. Amherst says this clearly.
6. **Send everything before the aid deadline.** The aid deadline can be different from the admission deadline. Dartmouth's aid documents are due on a different date from its application.
7. **Keep copies** and screenshots of everything you submit.

## Mistakes that cost students money

- Paying for the CSS Profile when the college did not ask for it yet.
- Missing the aid deadline because you only remembered the admission deadline.
- Using the wrong currency. Use your home currency where the college says so.
- Sending documents before the college's portal opens.
- Paying anyone to "secure" a scholarship. Amherst says it will never ask you to pay to be considered. The only fees are the application fee and the testing and CSS fees.
- Assuming "need-blind" means "free". It means the college does not look at your money when it admits you. You still need to complete the aid forms.

## Quick questions

### Does asking for aid hurt my chances?
At need-blind colleges, no. At need-aware colleges, it can. Check each college.

### Can I get aid and a merit scholarship together?
It depends on the college. Read its aid page.

### What if my parents have no payslips?
Ask the aid office what it accepts. Yale says checklist items can be changed or waived once documents are received.

### Is this only for US colleges?
No. But the CSS Profile and these words are mostly used by US colleges. Other countries use other systems.

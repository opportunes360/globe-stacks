Two documents appear in almost every scholarship application: letters from people who know you, and a short CV. Both are easy to do well if you start early.

## Recommendation letters

A recommendation letter is written by someone else, usually a teacher or school leader. It tells the panel what you are like when you are not talking about yourself.

### Who to ask

Choose people who know your work well and can give real examples:

- A **subject teacher** from a subject related to your course.
- Your **class teacher, head of department or head of school.**
- A **mentor, club leader or employer**, if they know your work well.

Pick someone who knows you over someone with an impressive title who barely knows you.

### How to ask

1. **Ask at least 3 to 4 weeks before the deadline.** Good letters take time.
2. **Ask politely and in person if you can,** then follow up in writing.
3. **Ask a clear question:** "Do you feel you know my work well enough to write me a strong recommendation?" This gives them an easy way to say no, which is better than a weak letter.
4. **Give them what they need,** in one tidy folder or one email:
   - the scholarship name and the **deadline**,
   - how they must send the letter (upload, email, or sealed envelope),
   - your CV,
   - your personal statement draft,
   - a short list of things you would like them to mention, like a project, a result or a time you led others.

### After they agree

- Remind them politely **one week** before the deadline.
- **Thank them** in person or in a message.
- Tell them when you get your result. They will want to know.

## Your CV

A CV is a **one-page summary** of you. For students, one page is enough.

### What to include

1. **Name and contact:** phone, email (use a sensible address like `firstname.lastname@gmail.com`), and town and country.
2. **Education:** school, years, and your results or grades.
3. **Achievements:** prizes, positions, top results.
4. **Experience:** projects, volunteering, jobs, leadership in clubs.
5. **Skills:** languages (with level), computer skills, anything useful.
6. **Referees** (optional): or say "available on request".

### How to write each point

Start with a strong verb and show a result:

- **Weak:** "Member of the debate club."
- **Stronger:** "Led the school debate club of 25 students; team reached the regional final in 2025."

### Common mistakes

- A long CV. Keep it to one page.
- A photo, unless the application asks for one.
- Fancy colours and many fonts. Plain and tidy is best.
- Mistakes in spelling or dates.
- Unprofessional email addresses.

### Saving it

Save your CV as a **PDF** with a clear name, for example `Firstname-Lastname-CV.pdf`. Keep a copy in your email and on your phone.

## Keep a "documents folder"

Make one folder (on your phone or email) with your CV, scanned certificates and transcripts, passport photo page, and your best personal statement draft. When a deadline appears, you will be ready.

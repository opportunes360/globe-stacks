Reading good examples is one of the fastest ways to learn how to write your own. This guide shows **three short sample statements** and explains what makes each one work. You will also see weak sentences turned into strong ones.

First, a very important rule.

> **Do not copy these samples.** The three students below are made up, and so are their stories. Your story is different, and an essay that does not sound like you will not help you. Use the samples to learn the *shape* of a good statement, then write about your own life.

If you have not read it yet, start with [how to write a personal statement](/guides/how-to-write-a-personal-statement/). It explains the structure. This guide shows that structure at work.

## How to use the samples

1. Read the statement once, quickly, like a reader would.
2. Read the comment after it. Notice what the writer did.
3. Ask yourself: "Where in my own life is there a moment like this?"
4. Close the page and write about your moment, in your own words.

Each sample below is only a **short extract** of about 150 to 200 words. A real statement is longer and follows the word limit on the application. Where a sample mentions "the programme", you would name the real course and one real thing about it, taken from its official page.

## Sample 1: The science student

*Amina is in Form Six. She studies physics, chemistry and biology and wants to study chemistry at university. (Made-up student.)*

> Last year the water at our school turned cloudy after the rains, and many students had stomach problems. Our teacher said we could not afford a laboratory test. So I asked if we could try our own. With a borrowed thermometer, some clean bottles and a method from a library book, three classmates and I tested samples from the tap, the tank and the nearby stream. We found the tank was the problem. The school cleaned it, and the sickness stopped within two weeks.
>
> That month I understood that chemistry is not only something in an exam. It protects people. I want to study chemistry so I can learn to test water properly, not just carefully. In the programme's first-year laboratory course I would finally use real equipment, and I would like to continue with environmental chemistry.

**What works**

- **It opens with a real event, not a slogan.** The reader sees the cloudy water at once.
- **It shows what she did and what changed.** She tested, found the cause, and the sickness stopped. That is proof, not just a claim.
- **It is honest about limits.** "Borrowed thermometer" and "library book" show she worked with little, which is a strength.
- **It links her past to the course.** The last lines name something specific she wants to study.

## Sample 2: The student from a small village

*Baraka grew up in a village far from the nearest town. He wants to become an agricultural officer. (Made-up student.)*

> My village has no electricity, and my secondary school is six kilometres away. I leave home at five in the morning and I study in the evening by my mother's small solar lamp. Some people feel sorry for me when I say this. I do not ask for that. Walking taught me patience, and the lamp taught me to use my time well.
>
> My family grows maize and beans. Every year some of the crop is lost because we do not know how to store it properly. When I read about better storage methods in a farming magazine, I tried one on our own harvest. We lost much less that season, and two neighbours asked me to show them how.
>
> I want to study agriculture so I can help farmers like my father keep what they grow. After I graduate I plan to return and work with farmers in my region.

**What works**

- **It tells the truth about his situation without begging.** "I do not ask for that" shows dignity. Readers respect it.
- **It turns hard conditions into evidence of character.** The walking and the lamp show discipline without using the word "hardworking".
- **It has a small, real result.** Less lost crop, and two neighbours asking for help. That is a project, even though it is small.
- **The plan is realistic.** Returning to work with farmers in his own region is clear and believable.

A small village is not a weakness in a statement. Many students from towns and cities have never needed to solve the problems you see every day.

## Sample 3: The student who led a project

*Neema started a reading club at her school. She wants to study education. (Made-up student.)*

> In Form Two I noticed that many students in the younger classes could not read a paragraph aloud, even though they were smart. Our school library had books, but nobody used them. I asked our headteacher for permission to start a reading club that met every Thursday after lessons.
>
> At first only four students came. I did not give up. I asked teachers to recommend easy books, and I made a chart so each member could see their progress. By the end of the year thirty-one students attended, and several teachers told me the club members were speaking more in class.
>
> Leading the club taught me that I enjoy helping someone understand something new. It also showed me how hard it is to teach well. I want to study education so I can learn the methods that I was only guessing at. My dream is to improve how reading is taught in schools like mine.

**What works**

- **Numbers make it believable.** "Four students" growing to "thirty-one" is easy to picture and easy to remember.
- **It shows how she led, not just that she led.** She asked permission, found books, made a chart. A leader is someone who does these steps.
- **She admits what she does not know yet.** "Methods that I was only guessing at" is honest and explains why she needs the course.
- **The ending connects to her goal.** It does not just stop.

## Before and after: turn weak sentences into strong ones

The same few changes improve almost every weak sentence. Name a real moment, give a detail, and show a result.

**1. Weak:** "I am passionate about computers."
**Strong:** "At fourteen I taught myself to fix the school's old computers using videos I downloaded at an internet cafe. Five of them work again."

**2. Weak:** "I want to help my community."
**Strong:** "I want to help my community, where most households still collect water from a shared well. I plan to study civil engineering so I can design safer water systems."

**3. Weak:** "I am a good leader."
**Strong:** "As head prefect I changed our cleaning rota so that every class shared the work equally. Complaints stopped within a month."

**4. Weak:** "This scholarship is a great opportunity for me."
**Strong:** "I am applying because the programme's research project in the third year matches the work I want to do on crop storage."

**5. Weak:** "I have faced many challenges in my life."
**Strong:** "When my father lost his job, I sold vegetables after school for a year so I could pay my own exam fees."

Notice that each strong sentence is **longer and more specific**, not more fancy. You never need big words. Plain, clear sentences are the best choice.

## What all three samples have in common

- They start with something that **really happened**.
- They show **what the student did**, not just what they feel.
- They include a **small result** (a number, a change, a reaction).
- They connect to **the course** they want to study.
- They look to **the future** in a realistic way.
- They sound like a **real 17-year-old** and not like a textbook.

## Common mistakes in samples (and in your own writing)

- **Copying a sample.** Even changing a few words is still copying. Write your own story from the beginning.
- **Making things up.** Do not invent a project or exaggerate. If you are asked about your essay later, you must be able to talk about everything you wrote.
- **Writing the same essay for every application.** Change the part about the programme each time, using details from its official page.
- **Starting with a cliché.** Avoid "Since I was a child I have always dreamed of...".
- **Trying to sound clever.** Short, clear sentences work better.

## A simple way to start writing yours

Take a sheet of paper and write answers to these questions. Do not worry about good sentences yet.

1. What is one time I solved a problem, even a small one?
2. What did I do, step by step?
3. What changed afterwards? Can I add a number?
4. What did I learn about myself?
5. Which course do I want, and what exactly in that course interests me?
6. What do I plan to do after I graduate?

Your answers to questions 1 to 4 can become your opening. Questions 5 and 6 become the ending. Then follow the structure in the [personal statement guide](/guides/how-to-write-a-personal-statement/).

## Checklist before you send

- [ ] Does my first sentence describe a real moment?
- [ ] Do I show what I did, and not only what I feel?
- [ ] Is there at least one result, with a number if possible?
- [ ] Do I mention something specific about the course or programme?
- [ ] Is everything in it true?
- [ ] Is it within the word limit?
- [ ] Did a teacher or another person read it and give me feedback?
- [ ] Is every word my own?

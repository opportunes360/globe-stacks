You have the scholarship. You have the visa. Now comes the part nobody explains well: **what happens to your money and your first days in a new country.**

This guide gives you a simple plan. We read official pages and university pages for it. Every number below comes from a page named in the Sources list at the end, and each one belongs to **one country or one university**. They are **examples** of what to expect, not rules for every country. Always check the official page of your own university and country.

If something is not on a page we read, we say **"not confirmed"**.

## 1. Know what your scholarship does and does not pay

Before you plan anything, open your award letter and answer three questions:

- Does it pay tuition, housing and food, or only some of them?
- Does it pay your flight, your visa fee and your insurance?
- Do you get a monthly allowance, and when does the first payment arrive?

Many scholarships do **not** pay for everything. For example, our [Berea College entry](/scholarships/berea-college/) says aid covers 100% of tuition, housing, food and fees, but admitted students also pay a **$2,200 deposit** (extra aid is available if you cannot pay it all). The official page does not say whether flights or the visa fee are covered.

So the first rule is simple: **do not guess what is covered. Write it down from your letter, and ask the scholarship office about anything unclear.**

## 2. Make a simple budget

A budget is a plan. It compares the money you expect to receive with the money you expect to spend each month. One Canadian college tells students to include all costs of student life, "not only tuition and housing."

Include: housing, food, transport, phone and internet, bills, books and study costs, health costs, warm clothes and bedding for your first month, a little for fun, and an **emergency amount** you do not touch.

### An example from the UK

The University of Exeter says a single student in Exeter or Cornwall "will need approximately £1,300-£2,000 a month" for basic living expenses. Its weekly examples are:

- Student halls (bills included): £150-£270 a week (self-catered)
- Groceries: about £60 a week
- Socialising: about £40 a week
- Public transport: about £18 a week

The same page reminds students that a **TV licence** costs £13.25 a month if you watch or record TV, including on-demand services. It also says to set money aside for bedding, kitchen things and warm clothes when you arrive.

Remember: these are numbers for **one city in one year**. Another city, or another country, will be different. Look for the "cost of living" page of your own university.

**Saving tips from the Exeter page:** buy own-brand food, shop with a list, cook in bulk, use student discounts and compare SIM-only phone plans.

## 3. Show you can pay: the proof-of-money rule

Some countries ask you to prove you can pay before they give a visa. The rules are different everywhere, so read your embassy's page. As an example, one UK university page says that for UK applications made on or after 12 November 2025, the Home Office asks students to show **£1,171 per month for up to nine months** (that is £10,539 for a course of 12 months or longer), plus tuition. The money must stay in the account for **28 days**.

The same page says that if a **government or scholarship agency fully sponsors you**, you do not need to show a bank balance. You need a recent letter on official letterhead with your name, the sponsor's name and contact details, how long the sponsorship lasts, and a statement that fees and living costs are covered.

That is the UK only. For other countries, check the embassy page. See our [student visa basics](/guides/student-visa-basics/) guide.

## 4. Get a bank account

You will need a bank account abroad for rent, phone bills, shopping and sometimes your scholarship allowance. Some points to know:

- **Banks check who you are.** In Canada, the Financial Consumer Agency of Canada says you must show **original** identification, not copies. A foreign passport is on its approved list. The same page says people who are not citizens may need to visit a branch in person.
- **If a bank refuses, you have rights in Canada.** The bank must give you a written notice that includes its complaint process.
- **It can take time.** The University of Leicester (UK) says you need your passport and a "bank letter" from the university, which is given after you finish your visa checkpoint. It says opening an account can take **up to four or five weeks** because of security checks.
- **Do not carry a lot of cash.** Leicester suggests bringing a credit or debit card that works in the UK instead of large amounts of cash, and about **£1,000** (separate from tuition and housing fees) for a single student in university housing. It says individual needs vary.
- **Read the account agreement.** The Canadian page tells you to read it carefully, ask about anything unclear and keep a copy.
- **Ask your university's international office** which banks they work with and what letters they can give you.

**Not confirmed:** how to send money from Tanzania to a student abroad (bank rules, fees and limits). We did not read an official Tanzanian page on this. Ask your own bank and check the Bank of Tanzania's website before you send or carry money.

## 5. Understand health insurance

In a new country, a hospital visit can be expensive if you are not covered. Every country is different. Two examples:

**UK.** Cardiff University says non-EEA students applying for a visa for more than six months pay the **Immigration Health Surcharge**, which the page gives as £776 a year, paid online when you apply for the visa. This gives you access to the NHS (the public health service): GP visits and hospital treatment. The page says you may still pay extra for **dental care, eye care and illnesses you already had before arriving**. Prices change, so check the current amount on the UK government page.

**Canada (UBC Okanagan campus).** The UBC page says to apply for the provincial plan (MSP) in your first week. It also says there is a **three-month waiting period** before MSP starts, so you should check that you have the extra insurance (iMED) to cover those first three months. Premium amounts are not on that page, so: **not confirmed**.

What to do:

1. Read your university's insurance page before you travel.
2. Ask your scholarship office if insurance is already included.
3. Find out what happens in the **first weeks**, because some plans do not start straight away.
4. Keep your insurance card or number on your phone and on paper.

## 6. Your first-week checklist

Use this list. It is based on UBC Okanagan's arrival page, which you can adapt to your own university. Some items will be different where you go.

**Before you leave Tanzania**

- Make photocopies **and** phone photos of your passport, visa, admission letter, scholarship letter and insurance papers. UBC's page tells students to copy documents and make digital backups.
- Save your university's international office contact and your first address.

**First week**

- Go to your university's arrival or welcome desk.
- Get your student ID card.
- Register for health cover (see section 5).
- Start opening a bank account.
- Get a local phone plan or SIM.
- Save the emergency numbers in your phone. At UBC Okanagan, the page lists **911** for police, fire and ambulance in Canada, plus the campus emergency and non-emergency numbers.

**First month**

- Locate the campus health and counselling services, the nearest clinic or pharmacy and the nearest hospital.
- Add your emergency contacts in the university system. UBC says this is required for every student.
- Enter your visa and permit expiry dates somewhere you will see them. UBC has a reminder system for this.
- Pay your tuition or fees by the deadline if the scholarship does not pay them. UBC's page says to check the deadline. It does not give the date on that page.
- Find out if your country has a tax or ID number you must apply for. In Canada, UBC's page says to apply for a Social Insurance Number within the first three weeks.

## 7. Staying safe

- Save the emergency number and the campus security number.
- Keep your passport and visa documents safe, and keep copies. Do not share them or your bank details with people you do not know.
- Remove your phone number from public social media profiles. One university scam page advises this.
- If something feels wrong, go to your international student office. They deal with these cases.

## 8. Avoiding scams

New students are a common target. Two US universities (University of Texas at Austin and Santa Clara University) describe the same patterns. Here is what they say:

**Common scams**

- **Fake officials.** Someone calls and says they are from immigration, the tax office, the police or even your embassy. They say you have a problem with your visa or an unpaid fee, or that you will be arrested.
- **Fake bank calls or texts** saying there is a problem with your account.
- **Fake jobs.** You are asked to pay a fee, or to deposit a cheque and send part of it back.
- **Love or "friendship" scams** that end with a request for money or a "crypto investment."
- **Housing scams.** Santa Clara lists housing among common scams, but its page does not describe how they work, so we do not explain the details here.

**Red flags**

- Pressure to act **now**, fear, or secrecy ("do not tell anyone").
- Payment by **gift cards, wire transfer, cryptocurrency or payment apps**.
- Someone asking you to move the chat to a private app.
- Anyone asking for your full password, PIN or one-time codes.

The UT Austin page puts it clearly: "Government agencies will never demand payment through gift cards, wire transfers, or cryptocurrency," and "You should never pay money to secure employment."

**What to do**

1. **Hang up** or stop replying.
2. **Check through an official channel.** For a bank, use the number on the back of your card. Do not click links in a message.
3. **Tell your international student office straight away**, even if you feel embarrassed. They will help you with next steps.
4. Look at your bank account often and set alerts if your bank offers them.

### A warning about visa "helpers"

The Government of Canada said in June 2023 that some students faced removal from Canada because their study permit applications included **fake acceptance letters**, and that some had been misled by people pretending to be immigration helpers. It told students to check that they have an acceptance letter from a recognised school and to use the official immigration website.

The lesson is the same everywhere: **no agent can guarantee a place or a visa, and you should never pay someone to "get you a scholarship."** Our guide on [how scholarships work and how to spot scams](/guides/how-scholarships-work-and-how-to-spot-scams/) has a full checklist.

## 9. A final checklist

- [ ] I know exactly what my scholarship pays for, from my award letter.
- [ ] I made a monthly budget and an emergency amount.
- [ ] I know whether my visa needs proof of money.
- [ ] I have copies of all important documents, on paper and on my phone.
- [ ] I know how my health cover works and when it starts.
- [ ] I have a plan for a bank account and a way to pay for the first weeks.
- [ ] I saved the emergency numbers and my university's international office contact.
- [ ] I know the scam red flags.
